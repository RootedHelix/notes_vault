# AI Recommendations

Treat it less like “I need to impress a CIO” and more like “I need to make a strong, credible first impression, learn, and leave with a clear next step.”

Given where you are now, your best angle is not pretending to be more advanced than you are. Your best angle is being early-career, technically serious, clearly motivated, and unusually intentional.

For a 30 minute meeting, I’d structure your approach like this:

### 1. Your goal is not to ask for a job immediately

Your real goals are:

- build trust
    
- show seriousness and direction
    
- learn how someone at that level thinks
    
- make it easy for them to help you later
    

If a job opportunity comes up, great. But if you go in too aggressively trying to “get hired,” it can feel premature. A better outcome is that they leave thinking, “This person is worth keeping in mind.”

### 2. Lead with a concise personal story

You need a very clean 60–90 second introduction.

Something like:

> - I’m early in my career and working toward embedded systems/software engineering. I have about two years of university, programming experience, and an internship background, and lately I’ve been spending a lot of time building my technical foundation through embedded and systems-level projects.
> -  I’m trying to be deliberate about how I grow from here, both in the kinds of roles I pursue and in the skills I build.
>  -  I wanted to use this meeting to learn from your perspective on technology leadership, career development, and where someone with my background can best position themselves.

That works because it is honest, ambitious, and grounded.

### 3. Your credibility comes from seriousness, not seniority

You do not need to sound like an expert. You need to sound like someone who is:

- disciplined
    
- teachable
    
- building real skills
    
- thoughtful about long-term direction
    

That means:

- speak clearly about what you are working on
    
- avoid exaggeration
    
- do not oversell projects
    
- show that you understand you are still developing
    

A CIO will usually respect self-awareness more than inflated confidence.

### 4. Have 2–3 concrete things you can talk about

Be ready to discuss a few real examples from your background. Keep them simple and structured:

- what it was
    
- what you were responsible for
    
- what you learned
    
- what direction it pushed you toward
    

For you, that might be:

- your embedded systems learning and firmware architecture work
    
- a project where you had to understand hardware/software interaction
    
- your internship and what it taught you about working in a real environment
    

Do not ramble technically unless they ask. Keep it high level first.

A useful formula:

> I’ve been working on X. What’s been interesting is Y. The main thing I’ve learned is Z.

### 5. Ask strong questions

This meeting will go well if the other person talks too. Ask questions that show maturity.

Good examples:

> - - “Are there areas in technology organizations where someone with my background could contribute while continuing to grow toward more technical engineering work?”

- “From your perspective, what separates early-career people who grow quickly from those who stall?”

    
- “If someone wants to move toward embedded/software systems work, what kinds of roles or environments build the strongest foundation?” -- >> > When you look at early-career technical people, what kinds of first roles tend to set them up best for long-term growth in engineering or systems-focused work?

    
- “When you meet younger technical people, what makes them memorable in a good way?”


- “How do you think about hiring potential versus experience?”


- “What would you focus on if you were in my position over the next 12 to 24 months?”





- “How do you evaluate new technologies like AI without getting distracted by hype?”
    

Those are much better than generic questions like “Do you have any advice?”

### 6. Be careful about how you frame your ambitions

Do not come across as scattered or desperate. You want to seem focused but open.

A strong framing is:

> I’m trying to make smart decisions early, even if the first role isn’t perfect, as long as it helps me build toward strong technical work.

That shows patience and maturity.

### 7. If job opportunities come up, handle it lightly

If they mention roles or opportunities, respond with interest but not pressure.

For example:

> That sounds interesting. I’d definitely be open to learning more if you think there might be a fit.

Or:

> I’m very interested in opportunities that let me keep building technical depth, especially if there’s room to learn from strong engineers.

That is better than immediately pushing your resume or asking for referrals too hard.

### 8. Things to avoid

Avoid:

- trying to sound overly technical to prove yourself
    
- talking too long without pause
    
- apologizing for being early in your career
    
- saying you’ll do “anything” for a job
    
- making the meeting all about your need
    
- acting as if one conversation should lead to a big opportunity
    

Also avoid underselling yourself. You are not “just a beginner.” You are someone actively building capability.

### 9. Best-case tone

The tone you want is:

- respectful
    
- curious
    
- direct
    
- calm
    
- appreciative
    

Basically: serious young engineer, not salesman, not fanboy, not someone begging for a break.

### 10. Close the meeting well

In the last few minutes, say something like:

> I really appreciate your time. This has been helpful for me in thinking more clearly about how to approach the next stage of my career. Based on what you’ve seen, I’d be grateful if you’d keep me in mind if there are roles, teams, or people you think I should speak with.

That gives them a natural opening to help.

### 11. After the meeting

Send a short thank-you note the same day.

Something like:

> Thank you again for taking the time to speak with me today. I really appreciated your perspective on career growth and technology leadership. Our conversation gave me a clearer sense of how to think about my next steps, and I’m grateful for your advice. I’d be glad to stay in touch.

### 12. Your mindset going in

Your standard for success should be:

- Did I make a strong impression?
    
- Did I learn something useful?
    
- Did I create a path for future contact?
    
Not:

- Did I get offered something immediately?
    

That mindset will make you come across much better.
